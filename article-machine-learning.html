Can Machine Learning Help Us Unlock Earth's Geological Past?

How artificial intelligence is helping scientists interpret volcanic ash deposits, identify ancient eruptions and reconstruct Earth's geological history.

By Elliot May 10 November 2026 Approximately 7 minute read

Imagine discovering a microscopic fragment of volcanic glass buried within a sediment core recovered from the ocean floor. It may be only a few hundred micrometres across, yet its chemical composition could hold clues to an eruption that occurred hundreds of thousands of years ago.

The challenge is working out where it came from. Which volcano produced it? When did the eruption occur? And can it be matched to deposits found elsewhere?

These are questions that scientists have been investigating for decades. Now, machine learning is providing new ways to approach them, helping researchers recognise complex chemical patterns and investigate geological records that might otherwise remain difficult to interpret.

A geological fingerprint hidden in volcanic ash

Explosive volcanic eruptions can eject enormous quantities of fragmented material into the atmosphere. This material, known as tephra, can travel considerable distances before settling across landscapes, lakes and oceans.

Over time, these deposits become preserved within sedimentary sequences. Individual layers can provide valuable chronological markers, allowing scientists to compare records from different locations and establish the relative timing of environmental events.

This is the basis of tephrochronology: the use of volcanic ash layers to investigate and correlate geological, archaeological and environmental records.

However, identifying an ash deposit is not simply a matter of recognising its colour or appearance. Two deposits may look almost identical while originating from entirely different volcanoes.

Instead, scientists often examine the chemical composition of volcanic glass. The glass preserves information about the magma from which it formed, including the relative abundance of different chemical elements.

A volcanic ash deposit can carry a chemical fingerprint that helps distinguish it from deposits produced by other eruptions.

Major elements such as silicon, aluminium and iron provide important information about magma composition. Trace elements, present in much smaller concentrations, can provide additional distinctions between volcanic sources that might otherwise appear chemically similar.

The difficulty is that these chemical fingerprints are multidimensional. Comparing just two elements at a time can overlook important differences hidden elsewhere in the dataset. As the number of measurements increases, interpreting the relationships between samples becomes increasingly challenging.

Teaching computers to recognise volcanic sources

This is where machine learning becomes useful.

Rather than relying entirely on manually comparing individual chemical measurements, supervised machine learning algorithms can be trained using examples of volcanic ash whose origins are already known.

Each sample is described by a collection of geochemical measurements. These measurements form the input data, while the known volcanic source provides the answer the model is being trained to recognise.

During training, the algorithm learns patterns that distinguish one volcanic source from another. Once trained, it can examine an unfamiliar sample and estimate which known volcanic source is most consistent with its chemical composition.

Importantly, this is not one single approach. Different algorithms identify patterns in different ways. Decision-tree methods, random forests, neural networks and other classifiers can each capture different relationships within complex data.

Combining several algorithms into an ensemble allows their predictions to contribute to a shared result. Rather than relying on the judgement of one model, the ensemble can draw on several different ways of interpreting the same geochemical evidence.

From thousands of measurements to ancient eruptions

Research in Alaska has provided an important demonstration of how these techniques can be applied to real geological problems.

In a 2019 study published in the Journal of Quaternary Science, Bolton and colleagues evaluated 11 supervised machine learning algorithms using nearly 2,000 electron microprobe measurements of volcanic glass. Their dataset represented 10 volcanic sources across Alaska.

Artificial neural networks and random forests performed particularly well, achieving accuracy and kappa scores greater than 0.96 in their evaluation. The researchers also found that combining these approaches into an ensemble improved performance.

When applied to volcanic ash deposits from Eklutna Lake in south-central Alaska, the combined model produced predictions consistent with traditional methods, helping narrow down the likely volcanic origins of the deposits.

The research demonstrated that machine learning could accelerate the process of comparing volcanic glass compositions, while supporting rather than replacing established geological interpretation.

Building on this work, Lubbers and colleagues published a study in 2023 in Geochemistry, Geophysics, Geosystems. Their approach used an ensemble of six different machine learning algorithms to investigate the origins of large tephra-producing eruptions across the Alaska-Aleutian arc.

One important development was the inclusion of trace-element information and elemental ratios. The researchers showed that these additional chemical features could help distinguish volcanic sources that appeared more similar when only major element concentrations were considered.

This matters because different volcanoes can produce magmas with overlapping major-element compositions. Without the additional information, a model might confidently assign an ash deposit to the wrong source.

By incorporating trace-element ratios, the researchers improved the ability of their models to distinguish between certain volcanic sources and to produce more informative predictions for unfamiliar deposits.

Looking back one million years

The potential of this approach becomes particularly interesting when researchers investigate geological records extending far beyond the period of reliable preservation on land.

In July 2026, Lubbers, Loewen and Wallace published a follow-up study investigating explosive volcanism in the Gulf of Alaska. The research used volcanic ash recovered from marine sediment cores, combining geochemical analysis, compositional data methods and machine learning to investigate the region's volcanic history.

The researchers examined 70 marine-core tephra samples and identified 37 distinct eruptive events across eight cores, extending the investigated record to approximately one million years.

Their analysis identified Mount Katmai as the most probable source for 15 of these events. Emmons Lake volcanic centre and Fisher Caldera were also identified as important potential sources.

These findings are significant because the geological record preserved close to volcanoes is not always complete. Glaciation, erosion and other processes can remove or obscure older deposits, making it difficult to reconstruct the full history of explosive volcanism from land-based evidence alone.

Marine sediment cores offer another perspective. They can preserve ash deposited far from its volcanic source, providing researchers with an additional archive of past eruptions.

By linking these distant deposits to likely volcanic sources, machine learning can help researchers investigate how volcanic systems have behaved over much longer timescales.

When the computer gets it wrong

Despite these promising results, machine learning is not a substitute for geological expertise. In fact, some of the most important findings from this research concern the limitations of automated classification.

A model can only learn from the examples available to it. If a volcanic source is poorly represented in the training data, or if an unfamiliar deposit comes from a source that has never been included, the model may struggle to identify it correctly.

There is also a risk of confusing confidence with correctness. A classifier may assign a high probability to a particular volcano even when the true source is not represented in its training dataset.

The 2026 study addresses this problem through the use of conformal prediction. Rather than always returning one definitive answer, this approach can produce a set of possible volcanic sources. Under the method's assumptions, the prediction sets are designed to include the correct known source at a specified confidence level.

This is an important distinction. A prediction containing several possible volcanoes is not necessarily a failed result. It may be a more honest representation of what the available evidence can establish.

The researchers also highlight examples where chemical similarities between volcanic sources make confident identification difficult. In one case, a sample's rare earth element profile differed sufficiently from the expected composition that the researchers considered whether it might represent a previously unrecognised compositional class.

This is where geological judgement becomes essential. Stratigraphy, mineralogy, petrology, field observations and knowledge of regional volcanic systems all provide context that a classification algorithm cannot independently replace.

A new tool for understanding Earth's history

The application of machine learning to tephrochronology illustrates a broader development across the Earth sciences. Researchers increasingly have access to large, complex datasets that are difficult to interpret using traditional approaches alone.

Machine learning offers a way to investigate patterns across many variables simultaneously, helping scientists identify relationships that might be difficult to recognise through manual comparison.

For palaeoclimate research, the implications are particularly interesting. Volcanic ash layers can provide chronological reference points within sedimentary records, helping researchers compare environmental changes across different locations.

More reliable correlations could therefore strengthen the chronological frameworks used to investigate past climate variability, environmental change and the development of Earth's landscapes.

But the real achievement is not that computers can now identify ancient volcanoes independently. It is that researchers can combine geochemical evidence, statistical methods and geological expertise to investigate questions that were previously much more difficult to answer.

Machine learning does not replace the geologist. It gives researchers another way to interrogate the evidence preserved within Earth's geological record.

As analytical techniques improve and geological datasets continue to expand, these methods may become increasingly valuable across volcanology, palaeoenvironmental reconstruction and the wider Earth sciences.

The fragments of volcanic glass preserved within ancient sediments may be microscopic, but the stories they help reveal are anything but.
