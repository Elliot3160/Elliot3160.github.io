01 / The foundations

What is the Quaternary?

The Quaternary is the most recent period of the geological timescale. It began approximately 2.58 million years ago and extends to the present day. It is defined by major environmental fluctuations, repeated glacial cycles and the evolution and dispersal of our own species.

A / Geological time

A period, not just an Ice Age

The Quaternary is a formal geological period within the Cenozoic Era. Although often associated with glaciation, it encompasses much more: changing climates, ecosystems, sea levels, landscapes and human histories.

B / An interconnected science

Many disciplines, one record

Quaternary scientists combine evidence from sediments, landforms, fossils, ice cores, cave deposits, soils and archaeological sites to reconstruct how environments changed through time.

Why study it? Quaternary records help us understand the natural range of climate variability, how ecosystems respond to environmental change, how landscapes develop, and the context of modern human activity.

04 / Climate history

Ice Ages & glaciation

Glaciation is one of the defining features of the Quaternary, but an Ice Age is not one uninterrupted freeze. The period contains repeated alternations between colder glacial conditions and warmer interglacials.

The basic cycle

Glacials and interglacials

During glacial stages, global ice volume generally increased, sea level fell and large ice sheets expanded across parts of North America and northern Eurasia. During interglacials, warmer conditions drove substantial ice retreat and sea-level rise.

These changes were not uniform everywhere. Regional temperature, precipitation, ocean circulation and landscape responses varied, which is why scientists compare many different records.

The mechanisms

Milankovitch cycles

Changes in Earth's orbit and orientation alter the seasonal and geographical distribution of incoming solar radiation. The key orbital variations are eccentricity, obliquity and precession.

Orbital variations pace important aspects of Quaternary climate change, while feedbacks involving ice, oceans, vegetation and greenhouse gases amplify or modify the response.

Ice on the landscape

Ice sheets and glaciers

Ice sheets are vast bodies of ice that flow under their own weight. Valley glaciers are more topographically confined. Both erode, transport and deposit sediment, creating distinctive landforms and sediment sequences.

Evidence includes moraines, drumlins, glacial striae, till, outwash plains and meltwater channels. Interpreting them requires attention to their setting and relationships, not just their shape.

A changing sea

Sea-level change

When water is stored in continental ice sheets, global mean sea level falls. When ice melts, sea level rises. Local relative sea level is also affected by land movement, including glacial isostatic adjustment.

Raised beaches, submerged landscapes, marine terraces and coastal sediments can preserve evidence of former shorelines.

Key idea: orbital forcing helps pace glacial cycles, but the climate system responds through interacting feedbacks. No single mechanism explains every regional change or every detail of the Quaternary record.
05 / Past environments

Reconstructing past climates

We cannot measure the temperature of a lake or an ocean thousands of years ago directly. Instead, Quaternary scientists use proxies: measurable properties of natural archives that respond to environmental conditions.

Biological archives

Pollen and plant remains

Pollen grains are produced in enormous quantities and can be preserved in lake mud and peat. Their morphology can often identify the plant group that produced them.

Changes in pollen assemblages can indicate shifts in vegetation, treeline position and regional climate. Interpretation must consider pollen productivity, dispersal, preservation and the size of the source area.

Marine archives

Foraminifera and isotopes

Marine sediments contain the remains of organisms such as foraminifera. Their shells preserve geochemical signals, including oxygen-isotope ratios that reflect a combination of seawater temperature and global ice volume.

With calibration and supporting evidence, these records help reconstruct ocean conditions and changes in the global climate system.

Frozen archives

Ice cores

Ice sheets preserve annual to multi-annual layers, trapped air bubbles, dust and chemical signals. These provide evidence about past atmospheric composition, temperature and environmental conditions.

Ice-core chronologies can be exceptionally detailed, although layer counting, ice flow and the age difference between ice and enclosed gas must be considered.

Lake and peat records

Sedimentary sequences

Lake sediments and peat accumulate through time, preserving changing combinations of mineral matter, organic material, microfossils and geochemical signals.

A core is not automatically a climate history: scientists establish the depositional context, assess disturbance and build a robust age-depth model before interpreting change.

Proxy caution: a proxy is rarely a direct thermometer. It may respond to several environmental variables, and its meaning depends on calibration, preservation, chronology and the setting from which it was collected.
06 / Earth surface processes

Landscapes & sediments

Quaternary landscapes are archives of process. Their landforms and deposits record the movement of ice, water, wind and sediment, as well as changing vegetation, sea level and permafrost conditions.

Glacial environments

Deposits left by ice

Glaciers can deposit poorly sorted diamicton as till, while meltwater sorts and layers sediment into outwash, sandur and glaciofluvial terraces. Ice-contact environments can produce complex, rapidly changing sequences.

Landforms such as moraines, drumlins, eskers and kettle holes help reconstruct former ice margins, flow directions and meltwater pathways.

River systems

Fluvial landscapes

Rivers erode, transport and deposit sediment. Channel form, grain-size distributions, sedimentary structures and terrace sequences can reveal changes in discharge, sediment supply, vegetation and base level.

River terraces may preserve former floodplains at different elevations, but their age and origin need to be established rather than assumed from height alone.

Wind and cold climates

Loess, dunes and periglacial features

Wind can transport fine sediment to form loess deposits and build dune systems. In cold, ice-rich ground, freeze-thaw processes and ground-ice dynamics create patterned ground, involutions and other periglacial structures.

These deposits and features can reveal past aridity, wind regimes, seasonality and the presence of permafrost.

Reading a section

Stratigraphy in the field

Field interpretation starts with careful description: lithology, grain size, sorting, clast shape and fabric, sedimentary structures, contacts and deformation.

Those observations are then used to develop and test a depositional interpretation. A sediment log records evidence; the environmental interpretation is a reasoned explanation of that evidence.

07 / Life through time

Fossils, ecosystems & extinction

Quaternary fossil records reveal how plants and animals responded to repeated environmental change. They also help us understand the changing distribution of habitats and the ecological consequences of human activity.

Megafauna

Life in Ice Age worlds

Quaternary faunas included mammoths, woolly rhinoceroses, cave bears, giant deer and many other large mammals. Their distributions shifted as habitats expanded, contracted and fragmented.

Fossils, ancient DNA and archaeological evidence help investigate their ecology, population histories and interactions with people.

Vegetation history

Changing ecosystems

Plant communities changed as temperature, moisture, seasonality and atmospheric conditions shifted. Pollen, plant macrofossils, charcoal and biomarkers provide complementary evidence.

Ecological responses can lag behind climate change, and local topography or soils can create refugia where species persist.

Extinction debates

Why did some species disappear?

Late Quaternary megafaunal extinctions occurred at different times in different regions. Research examines climate change, habitat transformation, human hunting and other pressures.

The balance of causes is debated and likely varied by species and region; simple single-cause explanations rarely capture the complexity of the evidence.

Microfossils

Small organisms, big insights

Diatoms, ostracods, foraminifera and other microfossils can be highly sensitive to water chemistry, salinity, nutrient status and habitat conditions.

Because they are often abundant and respond to environmental gradients, microfossils can provide detailed records when their ecology and preservation are understood.

Think across scales: a fossil assemblage reflects more than climate. Preservation, transport, sampling, habitat preference and ecological interactions all influence what appears in the record.
08 / People & environments

Human evolution & the Quaternary

The Quaternary contains the evolution and global dispersal of Homo sapiens, alongside the histories of other hominins. Archaeological evidence sits within changing landscapes, climates and ecosystems.

Deep human history

Hominins through time

The Quaternary witnessed the emergence and diversification of several hominin groups. Fossils, stone tools and other archaeological traces provide evidence of anatomy, behaviour and technological change.

Chronology is crucial: archaeological interpretations depend on establishing the age and context of finds, often by combining multiple dating approaches.

Movement and adaptation

Migration and changing environments

Climate and environmental change influenced the availability of habitats, water and resources. Human populations moved, adapted and developed technologies in response to varied local conditions.

Migration was not a simple one-way journey; it involved repeated dispersals, population interactions and regional histories.

Archaeological context

Sites are landscapes

Archaeological material is often preserved within sediments shaped by rivers, caves, lakes, dunes or glacial processes. Understanding site formation helps distinguish human activity from natural movement and redeposition.

Geoarchaeology connects sedimentology, geomorphology and archaeological interpretation.

The recent past

From foraging to farming

During the Holocene, many regions saw the development and spread of agriculture, settlement and increasingly complex societies, though these changes occurred at different times and in different ways.

Archaeological and palaeoenvironmental records together help explore how people transformed, and were shaped by, their environments.

09 / Reading the record

How do we investigate the past?

The Quaternary is recent enough to preserve a remarkably diverse range of environmental archives. Each archive records different aspects of past conditions, and each has its own strengths and limitations.

01

Sedimentology

Grain size, sedimentary structures and lithology help identify depositional environments and processes.

02

Pollen analysis

Fossil pollen preserved in lake and peat sediments provides evidence of past vegetation and ecological change.

03

Tephrochronology

Distinctive volcanic ash layers can provide time-parallel marker horizons for correlating sediment records.

04

Geochronology

Radiocarbon, luminescence and other dating methods establish chronological frameworks for environmental records.

05

Stable isotopes

Isotopic measurements in materials such as foraminifera and ice can reveal aspects of past climate and environments.

06

Micropalaeontology

Microfossils, including foraminifera and diatoms, can indicate past water conditions and environmental change.

07

Geomorphology

Landforms such as moraines, eskers, terraces and dunes preserve evidence of former processes and environments.

08

Modelling & GIS

Spatial analysis and numerical models help test hypotheses and connect observations across regions and timescales.

11 / Field notes

A small Quaternary glossary

A few essential terms to help you navigate the literature and understand the language of Quaternary research.

Glacial

A period or condition characterised by the expansion of glaciers and ice sheets.

Interglacial

A relatively warm interval between glacial periods, during which ice sheets retreat.

Palaeoclimate

The climate of the geological past, reconstructed using natural archives and proxy evidence.

Proxy

Indirect evidence used to infer a past environmental variable, such as temperature or vegetation.

Stratigraphy

The study and interpretation of layered geological materials and their relationships in time and space.

Tephra

Fragmented material ejected during a volcanic eruption; preserved layers can help correlate and date records.

Chronology

The sequence and timing of events, often established by combining dating methods and stratigraphic relationships.
