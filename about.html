About Me

A curiosity about Earth's past, a passion for understanding its present, and an interest in shaping its future.

Hello, I'm Elliot.

I'm a geologist with a particular interest in the stories preserved beneath our feet. From ancient volcanic eruptions and changing climates to the geological conditions that influence modern infrastructure, I've always been fascinated by how much the Earth can tell us about its own history.

My background brings together physical geography, Quaternary science, geological research and applied ground investigation. I've spent time studying environmental change across geological timescales, developing computational approaches to geological problems, and working with the materials and conditions that engineers encounter in the field.

I created Fossils & Futures to bring these interests together: a place to explore Earth science, communicate research and make geological ideas accessible to people who are curious about the world around them.

My Academic Journey

My academic journey began at the University of Southampton, where I studied BSc Physical Geography between 2021 and 2024.

I graduated with First Class Honours. My undergraduate dissertation investigated river restoration using remotely sensed vegetation and moisture indices, including the Normalised Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Normalised Difference Moisture Index (NDMI).

This introduced me to the potential of combining environmental science, spatial data and quantitative analysis to understand how landscapes change.

I subsequently pursued an MSc in Past Climates and Environmental Change at Royal Holloway, University of London, graduating with Distinction in December 2025.

Through the Centre for Quaternary Research, I developed a deeper understanding of the processes that have shaped landscapes and environments throughout the Quaternary: the geological period encompassing the repeated glacial and interglacial cycles of the last 2.58 million years.

My postgraduate training covered palaeoenvironmental reconstruction, tephrochronology, volcanology, sediment analysis, geochemistry, statistical modelling and computational approaches to environmental science.

Research: Bringing Geology and Machine Learning Together

One of the most rewarding parts of my academic experience was my MSc dissertation, which explored the potential of machine learning for correlating Holocene silicic tephra.

Tephra consists of volcanic material deposited following explosive eruptions. Because individual eruptions can distribute distinctive chemical signatures across considerable distances, volcanic ash layers can provide valuable chronological markers in sedimentary records.

However, correlating these deposits is not always straightforward. Geochemical datasets can be complex, compositions are mathematically constrained, and different volcanic deposits may share overlapping characteristics.

My research investigated how machine-learning methods could help address these challenges.

I developed a computational classification workflow incorporating:

  • Major-element volcanic glass geochemistry.
  • Compositional data analysis and centred log-ratio (CLR) transformations.
  • Random Forest and XGBoost classification.
  • Ensemble soft-voting approaches.
  • Bayesian age-modelling and stratigraphic constraints.
  • Classification probabilities and uncertainty assessment.

A particular interest of mine was the application of compositional data analysis. Geochemical measurements are not ordinary independent variables: their constrained nature means that conventional statistical approaches can sometimes produce misleading relationships. Exploring log-ratio transformations helped me appreciate how important mathematical foundations are to meaningful geological interpretation.

The work demonstrated the potential for combining geochemical expertise, statistical methods and machine learning to support tephra correlation. It also reinforced an important principle: computational predictions are most useful when interpreted alongside geological context and independent evidence.

From Research to the Real World

While my academic background introduced me to the complexities of geological and environmental systems, working in industry has given me a different perspective on how that knowledge is applied.

My professional experience has involved ground investigation and geoenvironmental engineering, working with subsurface conditions, geological materials and the practical challenges associated with developing land and infrastructure.

This includes exposure to site investigation, geological logging, soil and rock characterisation, particle size distribution analysis, geotechnical interpretation and the wider process of understanding ground conditions.

I particularly enjoy the relationship between fieldwork and interpretation. A borehole log, laboratory result or geological map is more than a collection of data: each provides another piece of evidence that helps construct an understanding of what lies beneath the surface.

I find that the most interesting geological problems often sit between disciplines. Understanding the geological history of a site can be just as important as understanding its present-day engineering properties.

What Fascinates Me About Earth Science

My interests extend well beyond any particular academic subject or professional discipline.

I'm particularly interested in the Quaternary: the dramatic environmental changes that have shaped the landscapes we recognise today. Glacial advances, volcanic eruptions, changing sea levels, shifting ecosystems and the movement of sediments all leave evidence behind.

Palaeontology and fossil collecting offer another perspective on Earth's history, revealing how life has evolved alongside changing environments over geological timescales.

I also enjoy exploring the intersection of geology and technology. Programming in R and Python, working with GIS, and experimenting with statistical and machine-learning methods have become important parts of how I approach scientific questions.

And, perhaps unsurprisingly, I have a particular appreciation for the landscapes of Scotland, where geological history is often written remarkably clearly into the scenery.

Why Fossils & Futures?

I believe Earth science is one of the most interesting ways of understanding the world, but it can sometimes feel inaccessible outside universities and specialist professional circles.

Fossils & Futures is my attempt to make some of those ideas easier to explore.

I want this website to cover a broad range of topics, from introductory explanations of geological processes to more detailed discussions of scientific research, environmental change, engineering geology and emerging technologies.

Some articles will be written for readers encountering a subject for the first time. Others will explore technical questions, research methods and the scientific literature in greater depth.

My aim is to make the content informative, evidence-led and accessible without losing sight of the scientific detail that makes these subjects so interesting.

Above all, I want Fossils & Futures to be a place where curiosity is encouraged. You don't need a geology degree to appreciate the history beneath your feet.

Looking Ahead

Whether I'm investigating ground conditions, analysing geochemical data, exploring a geological landscape or developing a new computational tool, I'm motivated by the same underlying curiosity: understanding how the Earth works, how it has changed, and what that means for the future.

"The more we understand Earth's past, the better equipped we are to understand its present and prepare for its future."

Thank you for visiting Fossils & Futures. I hope you find something here that sparks your curiosity.
