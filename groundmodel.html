Why Better Ground Models Start With Better Data Workflows

Ground models are often treated as interpretive outputs, but their reliability depends on decisions made much earlier — in how factual data is captured, structured, checked and connected.

Technical insight Ground models AGS, QA/QC & data workflows By Elliot May

The central argument

Better interpretation starts before interpretation. A ground model is only as reliable as the factual evidence used to construct it. If logs, samples, laboratory schedules, AGS files and reports are fragmented, then geological judgement has a weaker foundation.

A ground model is often treated as the interpretive stage of a ground investigation. It is where exploratory hole records, laboratory results, groundwater observations, geological mapping, historical information and professional judgement are brought together to form a coherent understanding of the ground.

However, the reliability of a ground model is shaped long before the interpretive stage begins. It depends on the quality of the factual data, but also on how that data has been captured, transferred, checked, structured and preserved throughout the investigation.

Borehole logs, trial pit records, sample references, laboratory schedules, monitoring data, AGS files, drawings and factual report appendices are often treated as separate deliverables. In practice, they form a connected evidence base. If those connections are weak, the later interpretation becomes harder to support, even where the individual records appear complete.

Core principle

Better ground models do not simply come from collecting more data. They come from building workflows that allow factual data to remain connected, traceable and technically meaningful.

The ground model begins before interpretation

It is tempting to divide ground investigation into factual work on one side and interpretation on the other. That distinction is useful, but it can also hide how dependent interpretation is on the factual workflow that precedes it.

A ground model may rely on many different sources of information: exploratory hole logs, in-situ testing, laboratory results, groundwater observations, geological mapping, previous investigation data, site history and engineering constraints. Each dataset contributes something different, and each carries its own uncertainty. The interpretive task is not simply to collect these records, but to understand how they relate to one another.

That task becomes more difficult when factual data is fragmented. If laboratory results are not clearly linked to samples, if stratum names vary between logs and summary tables, or if groundwater observations are held separately from the rest of the dataset, then the interpreter must spend time reconstructing relationships that should already be clear within the workflow.

In that sense, data workflows are not only an administrative concern. They influence how efficiently and defensibly geological judgement can be applied.

Ground investigation is data-rich, but often workflow-fragmented

Ground investigation usually produces a considerable amount of information. Even a relatively small project can involve exploratory hole records, sample registers, laboratory schedules, geotechnical and chemical test results, groundwater monitoring, location plans, sections, photographs, AGS files and factual report appendices.

The difficulty is that this information often moves through several stages and formats before it is used for interpretation. A field description becomes a log. The log informs a laboratory schedule. Samples are linked to depths and exploratory hole references. Laboratory results are returned separately. AGS files are exported and validated. Drawings and appendices are assembled. A factual report is issued. Later, the same information may be used to develop a ground model and inform design assumptions.

Each of these transitions introduces the possibility of inconsistency. Sample references may differ between the log, schedule and laboratory certificate. Depth intervals may be copied incorrectly. The same material unit may be named slightly differently across exploratory holes or summary tables. AGS exports may not fully align with the final report. Revisions may be made in one part of the dataset without being reflected elsewhere.

None of these issues necessarily makes a project unusable. Many are small and can be resolved through review. The problem is that they create friction in the workflow and reduce confidence in the connections between records. A report can appear complete while still requiring significant technical reconciliation before the data can be used confidently for interpretation.

Why small inconsistencies matter

In ground investigation, data inconsistencies are not always purely clerical. Some can affect how the ground is understood.

For example, if a laboratory result is linked to the wrong sample depth, it may be assigned to the wrong stratum. If the same geological unit is described using different names across a site, it may become harder to correlate confidently between exploratory holes. If groundwater observations are incomplete or separated from the main factual record, the interpretation of hydrogeological conditions may become less robust. If laboratory results are not clearly traceable back to the original field description, the relationship between observed material and tested material becomes harder to assess.

Technical consequence

Strong data workflows do not replace professional judgement, but they make it easier to apply that judgement clearly and defendably.

These are not necessarily dramatic errors, but they matter because ground models require simplification. A ground model takes variable and incomplete ground information and represents it in a form that can support engineering decisions. That process involves judgement about which units should be grouped, where boundaries are inferred, which test results are representative, and where uncertainty should remain visible.

The quality of that judgement depends partly on the coherence of the factual evidence base. Strong data workflows do not replace professional judgement, but they make it easier to apply that judgement clearly and defendably.

AGS, QA/QC and traceability

AGS is important because it provides a structured format for exchanging ground investigation data. It allows information to move beyond static documents and into a form that can be checked, transferred, interrogated and reused.

However, AGS should not be treated as a complete solution on its own. A dataset can be correctly formatted while still requiring technical review. Validation can identify whether data has been structured in the expected way, but it cannot fully determine whether the geological relationships represented by that data are reasonable.

This is where QA/QC and technical review remain essential. A good workflow should make it easier to identify inconsistencies before they become embedded in the issued project record. A laboratory result should be traceable to a sample, the sample should be traceable to an exploratory hole and depth, and that depth should be traceable to the logged material and wider factual record.

Traceability matters because interpretation often depends on being able to move backwards through the evidence. If a test result appears unusual, the reviewer should be able to identify where it came from, what material was logged, how the sample was described, what depth it was taken from and whether any uncertainty exists around that record.

Why automation makes this more important

The increasing interest in AI and automation makes data workflows more important, not less.

Automated tools are only as useful as the datasets they interrogate. A tool that summarises laboratory results depends on those results being correctly linked to samples and strata. A tool that checks logging consistency depends on controlled terminology and structured records. A tool that supports factual reporting depends on the underlying data being traceable and internally consistent.

If automation is introduced into a fragmented workflow, it may help identify inconsistencies, but it may also reproduce them more efficiently if the underlying data structure is weak. This is why the discussion around AI in ground investigation should not begin with automation alone. It should begin with the quality of the factual workflow that automation would depend on.

Digital principle

The organisations that gain most from digital tools are unlikely to be those that simply adopt them fastest. They are more likely to be those that have already invested in reliable data structures, clear review processes and consistent technical governance.

What better data workflows would look like

Improving data workflows does not necessarily mean collecting more data. In many cases, the priority is to make existing data more connected, traceable and reusable.

Better workflows would place more emphasis on structured digital field capture, controlled terminology, consistent sample and test referencing, early validation, clear links between logs and laboratory results, defined QA/QC responsibilities, and visible audit trails. They would also preserve the value of ground investigation data beyond the issue of a factual report.

This is particularly important for ground modelling. A ground model benefits from data that can be interrogated, compared and traced. If exploratory hole records, laboratory results, groundwater observations and AGS data are connected clearly, the interpretive process becomes more efficient and more transparent.

The objective is not to make interpretation automatic. A ground model still requires geological understanding, engineering context and professional judgement. Better data workflows simply provide a stronger factual foundation for that judgement.

Conclusion

Better ground models start before the first interpreted section is drawn. They begin with the quality of the factual workflow: how data is captured, named, linked, checked, exported and preserved.

Ground investigation already produces large amounts of valuable information. The challenge is ensuring that this information remains connected and technically meaningful as it moves from site observation to factual reporting and, eventually, to interpretation.

As AI, automation and digital tools become more common in ground investigation, this will become increasingly important. New tools will only add value where the underlying data is reliable enough to support them.

The ground is complex enough.
Our workflows should help us understand it more clearly.
