Technical Briefing

Climate risk is not just about future hazards. It is about landscape response.

A practical briefing on climate-sensitive ground risk, long-term environmental memory, and why early-stage resilience planning should connect climate projections with geology, geomorphology, groundwater, soils and infrastructure consequences.

Core Proposition

A Better Way to Think About Climate-sensitive Ground Risk

Climate-risk planning often asks: what hazard is projected? This briefing argues that planners, consultants, developers and asset owners should also ask: how will the landscape and ground respond?

A flood is not only a water-depth problem. A drought is not only a water-resource problem. A wet winter is not only a rainfall statistic. Each hazard interacts with landform, geology, superficial deposits, groundwater, vegetation, drainage, infrastructure and previous landscape history.

This is the central argument: climate hazards become decision-relevant risks when they interact with physical systems. Understanding that interaction requires more than modern observations and future climate projections. It also requires longer environmental memory.

The useful shift Move from “is this area exposed to a climate hazard?” to “what physical response should we expect if that hazard intensifies?”

Executive Summary

Executive Summary

Climate resilience planning has a memory problem. Decisions about drought, flooding, water resources, land use and infrastructure resilience are often made using recent observations, current regulatory datasets and future climate projections.

These are essential, but they do not always capture the longer environmental history of a region. For physical climate risk, the instrumental record can be too short to represent the full range of natural variability, recurrence, persistence and landscape response.

This matters because climate hazards are not only meteorological events. They interact with catchments, soils, superficial deposits, groundwater, vegetation, slopes, drainage systems and infrastructure.

Palaeoenvironmental and geomorphological records can help identify how landscapes have responded to past droughts, wet phases, flooding, erosion, vegetation shifts, hydrological stress and abrupt climatic transitions. Used carefully, this evidence can complement climate projections and modern monitoring by extending the timescale over which risk is understood.

Key message The past is not a direct forecast, but it is a critical source of information about variability, thresholds and landscape response.
Why This Matters

Why This Is Relevant

Climate-risk work can become too abstract when it stops at hazard exposure. A site may be exposed to hotter summers, wetter winters or more intense rainfall, but the practical consequence depends on the ground and landscape those hazards act upon.

A clay-rich site may translate hotter, drier summers into shrink-swell movement. A floodplain may translate repeated inundation into soft-ground constraints, shallow groundwater, access disruption, scour or contaminant mobilisation. A relict periglacial slope may translate wetter winters or drainage change into shallow instability.

The relevance is therefore practical. This briefing gives a framework for moving from climate hazard to landscape response to planning, asset or construction consequence.

Chapter 1

The Problem with Short Climate Memory

Modern climate-risk assessment is often built around three main sources of evidence: recent observations, regulatory datasets and future climate change projections. Each is valuable. Recent observations show what has happened within living or institutional memory. Regulatory datasets provide standardised information for decision-making. Climate projections offer scenarios for future change.

However, these sources can still leave a gap. Many environmental systems operate over timescales longer than the instrumental record. Inevitably, the recurrence and magnitude of many environmental and climatic events are not fully captured by instrumental records.

A landscape may appear stable during the period for which modern observation exists, while having a longer history of sensitivity to drought, water stress, flooding or abrupt environmental change. Decisions based only on recent experience may underestimate the range of conditions a system can experience.

Core question How has this landscape responded to climatic stress before, and what does that imply for future decisions?
Chapter 2

The Practical Framework: Hazard, Landscape Response, Consequence

A climate-risk screen becomes more useful when it moves beyond hazard identification and asks how the landscape will respond.

The three-step logic

Step Question Example
1. Climate hazard What climate pressure is relevant? Drought, wetter winters, intense rainfall, heat, flooding
2. Landscape response How might the landscape or ground respond? Shrink-swell, erosion, softening, groundwater rise, slope movement
3. Planning or construction consequence Why does this matter? Foundation movement, access disruption, drainage failure, settlement, scour

The logic is simple, but it changes the quality of early-stage risk screening. It shifts the assessment from “is there a hazard?” to “what mechanism could turn this hazard into a decision-relevant risk?”.

Chapter 3

What Longer Environmental Memory Adds

Quaternary Science studies environmental change over the recent geological past, including the timescales over which modern humans, landscapes and climate systems evolved. Its value for climate-risk planning lies in its ability to extend environmental memory.

Palaeoenvironmental records can include:

  1. Lake and marine sediments
  2. Peat bogs
  3. Pollen microfossils
  4. Charcoal and fire history evidence
  5. Isotopic records including oxygen, deuterium and carbon
  6. Cave deposits
  7. River and slope deposits
  8. Glacial and periglacial landforms
  9. Sediments derived from volcanic eruptions

These records do not provide a one-to-one forecast of future conditions. Their value is in revealing patterns, thresholds and sensitivities that may be difficult to identify from instrumental records alone.

For climate resilience, the more useful question is not simply “what happened in the past?”. It is “what does the past reveal about how this particular landscape responds under stress?”.

Chapter 4

Why This Matters for Planning, Assets and Infrastructure

Planning decisions often treat climate hazards as external pressures: rainfall intensity, drought, heat, sea-level rise or flood probability. In practice, the consequence of a climate hazard depends heavily on the landscape it acts on.

The same rainfall event may produce different consequences depending on catchment shape, soil permeability, urbanisation, drainage capacity, antecedent moisture and floodplain storage. The same drought may have different implications depending on soil type, clay mineralogy, vegetation, groundwater dependency and foundation sensitivity.

The same period of wetter winters may have different effects depending on slope angle, superficial deposits, weathered rock, made ground and existing drainage pathways.

Better risk framing The most useful climate-risk question is often not “is this area exposed?”, but “what kind of physical response should we expect if this hazard intensifies?”
Chapter 5

From Climate Hazard to Ground and Planning Consequence

Climate hazards rarely act in isolation. They interact with ground conditions, land use and infrastructure systems.

Climate pressure Possible planning, land or asset implication
Prolonged rainfall deficit Water-resource stress, vegetation stress, soil moisture deficit, land-use pressure
Higher temperatures Increased evapotranspiration, heat stress, higher water demand, ecological pressure
Soil moisture deficit Shrink-swell behaviour, ground movement, service and foundation sensitivity
Wetter winters Higher groundwater, slope softening, reduced trafficability, earthworks constraints
Intense rainfall Surface water flooding, erosion, drainage exceedance, sediment mobilisation

A drought can increase soil desiccation, weaken vegetation cover and harden ground surfaces. Subsequent intense rainfall may then generate higher runoff, erosion or flash flooding. In clay-rich areas, seasonal moisture change may contribute to ground movement. On slopes, wetter winters may increase pore pressure and reduce shallow stability.

Planning therefore needs to ask not only whether a climate hazard is likely, but how the ground and landscape may respond.

Chapter 6

Climate-sensitive Landscapes Do Not All Evolve Equally

Different landscapes respond to climate pressures in different ways. Some systems may become more unstable under warming or wetter conditions. Others may adjust, revegetate, or partially stabilise over time. Some may alternate between stability and disruption depending on thresholds, storm events, drought cycles or land-use change.

This matters for construction because ground risk is rarely static. Slope deposits, floodplain alluvium, peat, clay-rich soils, made ground and weathered rock all have histories. Their future behaviour depends on how climate pressures interact with those histories.

Case Study 1

Periglacial and Cold-region Slope Systems

Core issue: warming, thawing, intense rainfall and drainage change can destabilise slopes with cold climate or relict periglacial histories.

Periglacial environments are shaped by cold-climate processes such as freeze-thaw, ground ice, solifluction, frost heave and seasonal thawing. In active periglacial terrain, warming can destabilise slopes by deepening the active layer, thawing ice-rich ground, removing ice cement and increasing pore water pressures.

In formerly periglacial regions, including upland Britain, relict periglacial deposits and landforms may still matter for ground models. Head deposits, solifluction sheets, blocky slope deposits and weathered rock mantles can be variable, loose, poorly sorted and sensitive to water movement.

Construction relevance

  • Variable slope deposits and uncertain ground models
  • Shallow instability in solifluction or head deposits
  • Rockfall or debris-flow hazard in steep terrain
  • Drainage sensitivity and erosion on access tracks
  • Uncertainty in founding conditions on relict slope deposits
  • Increased hazard where warming, intense rainfall or human disturbance interact
Screening question Is this slope stable because it is inherently strong, or because current climate, vegetation and drainage conditions are temporarily favourable?
Case Study 2

Floodplains and Alluvial Landscapes

Core issue: floodplains are not just flood zones; they are active sedimentary and groundwater systems.

Floodplains record previous phases of channel movement, inundation, overbank deposition, wetland formation, erosion and groundwater fluctuation. Vegetation can stabilise banks and surfaces in some settings, but increased flood magnitude, intense rainfall, sediment supply, channel modification or development pressure can also increase erosion, scour, deposition and drainage exceedance.

Floodplain stability is conditional. It depends on hydrology, sediment supply, vegetation, flood frequency, channel geometry, groundwater, artificial drainage and land use.

Construction relevance

  • Soft or compressible alluvium
  • Shallow groundwater
  • Organic soils or peat lenses
  • Buried channels and lateral variability
  • Flood inundation and access disruption
  • Erosion, scour and deposition
  • Contaminant mobilisation in urban or industrial floodplains
  • Drainage constraints where outfalls are affected by high water levels
Screening question Is this floodplain being treated as a static area of mapped flood probability, or as an active land system with sediment, groundwater and ground condition consequences?
Case Study 3

Clay-rich Soils and Drought-sensitive Ground

Core issue: drought is often treated as a water-resource problem, but in clay-rich ground it can become a ground-movement problem.

Clay-rich soils can shrink and swell as moisture content changes. Hotter, drier summers may increase soil moisture deficits, while wetter winters may promote seasonal rewetting. Vegetation, tree cover, impermeable surfaces, leaking services and drainage changes can further influence the moisture regime.

For construction and asset management, this matters because ground movement can affect foundations, roads, pavements, buried services, retaining structures and lightly loaded buildings. The risk is not simply “drought” in a regional sense. It is drought acting on a susceptible ground model.

Construction relevance

  • Seasonal foundation movement
  • Cracking or distortion in lightly loaded structures
  • Buried service movement or leakage
  • Pavement and road deformation
  • Vegetation-related moisture effects
  • Increased maintenance burden under hotter, drier summers
Screening question Are drought and soil moisture being treated as climate variables only, or as drivers of volume change, settlement and serviceability risk?
Chapter 7

From Static Ground Models to Climate-sensitive Ground Models

Traditional ground models are central to geotechnical and geoenvironmental assessment. They summarise expected ground conditions at a site, including geology, superficial deposits, made ground, groundwater, geomorphology and geohazards.

However, climate resilience requires a further question: how might these ground conditions respond under changing climatic pressures?

A climate-sensitive ground model does not replace conventional ground modelling. It extends it. It asks how future rainfall, drought, heat, groundwater variability and vegetation change could influence the behaviour of the ground over the design life or planning horizon of an asset.

Ground condition Conventional ground model question Climate-sensitive question
Clay-rich soils Are they shrink-swell susceptible? Could hotter, drier summers increase seasonal movement?
Relict periglacial deposits Are they variable or poorly sorted? Could rainfall, slope modification or drainage alteration reactivate movement?
Peat and organic soils Are they compressible or degrading? Could drying, rewetting or drainage change alter volume, carbon loss or stability?
Alluvium Is it soft, variable or compressible? Could flooding or groundwater change increase drainage, settlement or access risk?
Made ground What is the thickness and composition? Could intense rainfall worsen infiltration, instability or contaminant mobilisation?
Slopes Are they currently stable? Could wetter winters, drainage changes or vegetation loss increase shallow instability?
Granular deposits Are they loose, running or erosion prone? Could extreme rainfall increase erosion, scour or groundwater movement?
Key shift The key shift is from describing the ground as a static condition to understanding it as a climate-sensitive system.
Chapter 8

How This Changes Early-stage Due Diligence

A typical early-stage environmental or ground-risk screen may identify flood zones, geology, historical land use, made ground, mining risk, hydrogeology and environmental receptors. These are essential. But climate-sensitive screening adds another layer: it asks how those conditions could behave under future climate pressures.

This does not need to turn every desk study into a climate model. At early stage, the value is in asking better scoping questions and identifying where further assessment may be needed.

What changes in practice?

Standard early-stage question Climate-sensitive question
What is the mapped flood risk? How could flood frequency, groundwater and alluvial ground conditions interact?
What superficial deposits are present? Are they sensitive to wetting, drying, erosion, settlement or groundwater change?
Is made ground present? Could intense rainfall increase infiltration, instability or contaminant mobilisation?
Are slopes present? Could wetter winters or drainage alteration increase shallow instability?
Are clay soils present? Could hotter, drier summers increase shrink-swell movement?

For developers and asset owners, this helps identify whether further work should include drainage review, intrusive ground investigation, slope appraisal, flood-risk assessment, foundation-depth review, groundwater monitoring, SuDS design, climate adaptation planning or long-term maintenance allowances.

For consultants, it improves the quality of recommendations. Instead of saying only “further assessment may be required,” the report can explain why a specific climate-ground mechanism matters.

Chapter 9

Practical Questions for Climate-resilience Screening

A longer environmental memory does not need to make screening more complicated. It can improve the quality of the questions asked at the start of a project.

  1. Is the site, catchment or region already exposed to drought, flooding, erosion, groundwater stress or slope instability?
  2. Does the wider landscape show evidence of past sensitivity to wet/dry shifts, flooding, vegetation change or erosion?
  3. Are the soils or superficial deposits sensitive to moisture change?
  4. Could drought, wetter winters or intense rainfall change ground behaviour?
  5. Are current planning assumptions based mainly on recent observations?
  6. Could climate hazards combine, for example drought followed by intense rainfall?
  7. Are there land-use, ecological or infrastructure thresholds that may be crossed under future stress?
  8. What further data would reduce uncertainty: monitoring, intrusive investigation, modelling, walkover survey or specialist assessment?
  9. Has the ground model considered future climate pressures over the design life of the asset?
  10. Are landscape history and geomorphology being used to inform climate-risk interpretation?

These questions do not replace formal assessments. They help frame them better.

Chapter 10

Not a Replacement for Models — A Deeper Evidence Base

Palaeoenvironmental evidence should not be oversold. It is not a replacement for climate projections, hydrological modelling, engineering design, environmental regulation, site investigation or local monitoring.

Its role is complementary.

Evidence type Strength Limitation
Instrumental climate records Measured, precise, recent Often short relative to environmental variability
Climate projections Future-facing, scenario-based Uncertainty, resolution limits, model assumptions
Palaeoenvironmental records Long-term variability, thresholds, system response Indirect, proxy-based, interpretive uncertainty
Site-specific data Local and practical Often limited in time and spatial coverage
Policy/regulatory datasets Standardised and accessible May simplify complex local conditions

The strongest climate-risk decisions are not based on one evidence type alone. They combine modern observations, projections, local data, professional judgement and long-term environmental context.

Final Takeaway

Final Takeaway

Climate resilience is not only about modelling future hazards. It is also about understanding how landscapes have behaved under past stress.

Quaternary Science provides a longer environmental memory. It helps planners, consultants and asset owners avoid treating recent decades as the full range of possible conditions. Used carefully, palaeoenvironmental evidence can strengthen climate-risk screening by revealing variability, thresholds and landscape sensitivity.

The past is not a simple forecast. But it is evidence. For physical climate risk, ignoring that evidence leaves modern planning with a memory problem.

The next step for climate-resilience practice is not to replace existing models or assessments. It is to connect them better: future climate projections, modern monitoring, ground models, geomorphology and long-term environmental evidence should be read together.

Closing thought That is how climate risk becomes more than a map of hazards. It becomes an understanding of landscape response.
