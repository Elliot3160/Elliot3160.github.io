My MSc Dissertation

Machine learning and tephra classification
A publication-safe overview of research conducted at the Centre for Quaternary Research.

Project Overview

My MSc dissertation explored how modern data-driven methods can support the classification and correlation of volcanic tephra in Quaternary research. Tephra correlation has traditionally relied on expert comparison of glass shard geochemistry, which can be time-intensive and sensitive to interpretative bias.

This project examined whether machine-learning approaches can help improve consistency, transparency, and confidence in tephra classification workflows, particularly as geochemical datasets continue to expand.

Scientific Context

Tephra layers provide precise time-synchronous markers that allow palaeoenvironmental, archaeological, and climatic records to be aligned across regions. As analytical techniques such as EPMA become increasingly high-resolution, there is growing interest in computational tools that can assist interpretation.

This research was completed at the Centre for Quaternary Research (CQR) at Royal Holloway, University of London, within a wider context of methodological innovation in tephrochronology.

Research Aims

Rather than focusing on a single algorithm or dataset, the dissertation addressed broader conceptual questions, including:

  • How machine-learning models can assist tephra classification
  • Whether ensemble approaches improve interpretative stability
  • How geological context can inform probabilistic predictions

Specific datasets, parameters, and numerical results are intentionally not presented here to preserve the integrity and publishability of the work.

Methodological Approach (High-Level)

The research developed a conceptual machine-learning framework that integrates multiple modelling approaches with geological reasoning.

At a high level, the work explored ensemble-based classification, probabilistic interpretation, and uncertainty awareness, rather than optimising any single model.

This page does not include algorithms, workflows, figures, or code implementations.

Key Conceptual Insights

While quantitative results are withheld, the research highlighted several broader principles relevant to geoscience applications of machine learning:

  • Ensemble predictions can provide more stable interpretative outcomes
  • Geological context remains essential alongside statistical models
  • Uncertainty communication is as important as classification itself
  • Automated tools should support, not replace, expert judgement

Why This Matters

Robust tephra correlation underpins research across Quaternary science, including palaeoclimate reconstruction, volcanic hazard assessment, archaeological dating, and stratigraphic synchronisation.

This work demonstrates how computational approaches can contribute to these fields while remaining grounded in geological reasoning.

Future Directions

A manuscript based on this dissertation is currently in preparation. Future work will focus on methodological refinement, broader testing, and improving accessibility for researchers.

Alongside this, elements of the research are being explored through applied tools such as TephraLink, which translate high-level ideas into practical workflows.
