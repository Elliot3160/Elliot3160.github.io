My MSc Dissertation

Machine Learning Approaches to Tephra Classification
A publication-safe summary of my research at the Centre for Quaternary Research.

Project Overview

My MSc dissertation investigated how modern machine-learning methods can improve the classification and correlation of Icelandic tephra. Traditionally, tephra correlation relies on manually comparing glass shard geochemistry with reference datasets — a process that can be time-consuming, subjective, and limited by human interpretation.

This project explored whether algorithmic approaches can enhance consistency, reproducibility, and confidence in tephrochronological classification, especially as datasets continue to grow.

Research Context

Tephra layers act as instant geological time-markers, allowing scientists to synchronise palaeoenvironmental and archaeological records across vast distances. With the increased use of EPMA and high-resolution analytical datasets, there is a growing need for tools that can help automate or support tephra classification.

This project was completed within the Centre for Quaternary Research (CQR) at Royal Holloway, one of Europe’s leading groups in palaeoenvironmental reconstruction and volcanic ash studies.

Project Aims

The dissertation addressed three central questions:

  • Can machine-learning models accurately classify Icelandic glass shard chemistry?
  • Do ensemble models provide more stable predictions than individual algorithms?
  • How can stratigraphic age information refine probabilistic predictions?

These questions were approached conceptually without publishing any specific datasets, methods or figures — ensuring the work remains eligible for future journal submission.

Methodological Framework (High-Level)

To protect the publishability of the research, only a general outline of the methods is provided here. The project developed a machine-learning workflow combining:

  • Random Forests
  • XGBoost
  • Flexible Discriminant Analysis
  • Compositional data transformations (CLR)
  • Soft-voting ensemble averaging
  • Stratigraphic Bayesian age constraints
  • Uncertainty and class-probability diagnostics

The pipeline was designed to be transparent, scalable, and reproducible, with a focus on strengthening analytical tephrochronology.

Key Insights (Non-Numerical)

While specific accuracy results are withheld, the research produced several conceptual insights:

  • Ensemble models can improve stability and reduce overclassification.
  • Integrating age priors helps contextualise ambiguous samples.
  • Uncertainty assessment is as important as producing a class label.
  • Automated classification can complement, not replace, expert interpretation.

These findings align with current trends in geoscience, where hybrid approaches (expert + machine learning) offer powerful advantages.

Why This Research Matters

High-quality tephra correlation underpins a wide range of Quaternary and volcanic research, including:

  • palaeoclimate synchronisation
  • volcanic hazard assessment
  • archaeological dating
  • ice-core and marine-core alignment
  • Holocene & Pleistocene environmental reconstruction

The project demonstrates that machine learning can support these fields by providing reproducible, scalable classification tools.

Future Directions

A manuscript based on this dissertation is in preparation. Future development will focus on:

  • expanding datasets and class representation
  • integrating trace-element chemistry
  • advancing Bayesian ensemble modelling
  • building a researcher-friendly application or web tool
  • testing generalisability across volcanic provinces

The long-term goal is to create a robust open-source platform for tephra classification that can be used across the global tephrochronology community.
