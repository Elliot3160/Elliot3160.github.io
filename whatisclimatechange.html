Quaternary Climate Change Hub

Exploring 2.6 million years of Earth's climate history – and what it tells us about the present.

Introduction to Climate Change

Before climate change can be understood, it is essential to distinguish between weather and climate. Weather describes short-term atmospheric conditions such as temperature, rainfall, cloud cover and wind, which fluctuate over hours to days. Climate, in contrast, refers to the long-term statistical average of weather over decades or longer. Climate change therefore represents a sustained deviation from these long-term averages, rather than day-to-day variability.

Climate has changed naturally throughout Earth’s history, driven by processes such as orbital variations, volcanic activity, solar changes and feedbacks within the climate system. However, the modern pattern of warming is different: it aligns closely with the rapid rise in atmospheric greenhouse gases caused by human activities— primarily fossil fuel combustion, deforestation and industrial processes. The geological record provides compelling evidence for this. High-resolution climate proxies, especially those from the Quaternary period, clearly show when human influence began to push the climate outside its natural range of variability.

Over geological timescales, climate has never been static. Yet the rate and magnitude of warming observed since the late 19th century are exceptional when compared with the last several million years. The Quaternary period – the most recent 2.6 million years of Earth’s history – preserves a detailed archive of natural climate fluctuations. By examining these records, we can ask a crucial question: how does modern, human-driven climate change compare to past changes? The answer underscores just how unprecedented today’s changes are.

The Quaternary Period

The Quaternary is the most recent period in the geological timescale, beginning around 2.6 million years ago and continuing to the present day. It is traditionally divided into:

  • Pleistocene (2.6 million – 11,700 years ago): dominated by repeated glacial–interglacial cycles, large ice sheets and major shifts in sea level.
  • Holocene (last 11,700 years): the current interglacial, characterised by relatively stable climate conditions under which human societies, agriculture and complex civilisations developed.

During the Quaternary, ice sheets repeatedly advanced over large parts of North America, northern Europe and Asia, then retreated during warmer interglacial phases. These expansions and contractions were accompanied by changes in sea level exceeding 100 metres, major reorganisations of ecosystems, and migrations and extinctions of species.

Because it is geologically recent and exceptionally well recorded in ice, marine and terrestrial archives, the Quaternary offers a high-resolution window into how the climate system responds to changes in external forcing. It is the primary benchmark against which we judge whether current climate trends fall within, or outside, the bounds of natural variability.

Glacial–Interglacial Cycles

A defining feature of the Quaternary is the oscillation between cold glacial periods, when large ice sheets expanded, and warmer interglacial periods, like the Holocene. Glacials were several degrees cooler globally than present day, with lower atmospheric CO2 and extensive ice cover at high latitudes.

These cycles are paced primarily by Milankovitch forcing – predictable variations in Earth’s orbit and axial tilt that alter the distribution of incoming solar radiation (insolation) across the planet:

  • Eccentricity (~100,000-year cycles): changes in the shape of Earth’s orbit around the Sun.
  • Obliquity (~41,000-year cycles): changes in the tilt of Earth’s rotational axis.
  • Precession (~19,000–23,000-year cycles): the wobble of Earth’s axis, which alters the timing of seasons.

On their own, these orbital variations are relatively small perturbations to the planet’s energy balance. The large glacial–interglacial swings we observe in the geological record arise when this orbital pacing interacts with internal feedbacks in the climate system. These include changes in greenhouse gas concentrations, ice–albedo feedback, shifts in ocean circulation and vegetation cover.

The result is a characteristic pattern: slow build-up of ice sheets and gradual cooling, followed by comparatively rapid deglaciation. Understanding this behaviour is crucial, because it reveals how sensitive the climate system is to relatively small initial forcings once feedbacks are engaged.

How We Know: Climate Proxies

Direct instrumental measurements of weather and climate extend back only about 150 years. To reconstruct climate over Quaternary timescales, scientists rely on climate proxies – natural archives that indirectly record environmental conditions. Each proxy captures different aspects of the climate system and comes with its own uncertainties, so robust reconstructions typically combine multiple lines of evidence.

  • Ice cores – Cylinders of ice drilled from Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets contain trapped air bubbles that preserve past concentrations of CO2, CH4 and other gases. Stable isotopes of oxygen and hydrogen in the ice provide temperature-sensitive signals stretching back hundreds of thousands of years.
  • Marine sediments – Layers of sediment on the ocean floor record changes in microfossil assemblages, grain size and geochemical composition. Foraminifera shells and their isotopes (e.g. δ18O) are especially important for reconstructing ice volume and ocean temperature.
  • Speleothems – Cave deposits such as stalagmites grow layer by layer, incorporating isotopic and elemental signals that reflect past rainfall patterns and moisture balance on land.
  • Pollen records – Pollen grains preserved in lake and peat sediments document changes in vegetation communities through time, which can be linked to temperature and precipitation regimes.
  • Ancient DNA and biomarkers – Molecular traces of plants, animals and microbes in sediments provide additional insights into past ecosystems, sea-ice cover and biogeochemical cycles.

By combining these archives with robust dating methods, scientists can reconstruct past temperature, precipitation, ice volume, sea level and atmospheric composition. This integrated picture of Quaternary climate is essential for placing current changes in a long-term context.

Major Climate Events of the Quaternary

While glacial–interglacial cycles set the overall rhythm of Quaternary climate, several key events stand out as major reorganisations of the Earth system:

  • Mid-Pleistocene Transition (MPT) – Between roughly 1.2 million and 700,000 years ago, the dominant periodicity of glacial cycles shifted from ~41,000 years to ~100,000 years. This transition is likely related to evolving ice-sheet dynamics and feedbacks, rather than a simple change in orbital forcing.
  • Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) – Around 21,000 years ago, ice sheets were at their greatest extent, global sea level was more than 120 metres lower than today, and many mid- to high-latitude regions were colder and drier.
  • Deglaciation and Meltwater Pulses – The transition from the last glacial to the Holocene involved rapid warming, ice-sheet retreat and abrupt sea-level rise. Large meltwater pulses temporarily disrupted ocean circulation, with knock-on effects for regional climate.
  • Holocene Climate Variability – Although generally more stable than glacial times, the Holocene includes notable episodes such as the Holocene Thermal Maximum, the Medieval Climate Anomaly and the Little Ice Age, driven by a mix of solar variability, volcanism and internal dynamics.
  • Proposed Anthropocene – Many researchers argue that human activity since the Industrial Revolution has created a new geological interval distinct from the Holocene, marked by unprecedented greenhouse gas concentrations, widespread biodiversity loss and pervasive human modification of landscapes and biogeochemical cycles.

Each of these events provides a natural experiment in how the climate system responds to different types and magnitudes of forcing, offering valuable analogues – and contrasts – for understanding current and future change.

Today's Climate in Quaternary Context

When viewed against the backdrop of Quaternary climate history, modern warming stands out for both its speed and its cause. Past transitions from glacial to interglacial states involved global temperature changes of several degrees Celsius over thousands of years, initiated by orbital forcing and amplified by natural feedbacks. By contrast, global mean surface temperature has risen by more than 1 °C since the late 19th century, driven primarily by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions.

Ice-core records show that pre-industrial atmospheric CO2 fluctuated naturally between roughly 180 ppm during glacials and 280 ppm during interglacials. Modern concentrations lie far above this range, and the rate of increase is unprecedented within the Quaternary record. This places the current climate trajectory outside the envelope of “normal” Holocene variability.

Quaternary archives also highlight the existence of thresholds and potential tipping points – for example, in ice-sheet stability, ocean circulation and biosphere feedbacks. Once crossed, these thresholds can commit the Earth system to large, and in some cases irreversible, changes on human timescales, even if forcing is later reduced.

In this sense, the Quaternary is more than a backdrop. It is the context that allows us to recognise just how unusual present-day climate change is, and why rapid mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions is critical if we are to avoid committing future generations to a profoundly different climate state.
