Climate Change Through the Lens of Deep Time

Earth's climate has never been fixed. Over millions of years, ice sheets have advanced and retreated, oceans have risen and fallen, and ecosystems have repeatedly reorganised. The geological record allows us to investigate these changes — and to understand why the warming happening today is different.

01 · Foundations

What do we actually mean by climate change?

A cold winter does not disprove global warming, just as a particularly hot summer does not, on its own, establish a long-term climate trend. To understand climate change, we first need to distinguish between the weather we experience and the climate system that produces it.

Weather describes the atmospheric conditions experienced over relatively short periods: temperature, rainfall, wind, humidity and cloud cover. These conditions can change dramatically from one day to the next.

Climate, by contrast, describes the statistical behaviour of weather over much longer periods. Scientists commonly use 30-year intervals when calculating standard climate averages, although climate research also examines trends over centuries, millennia and geological timescales.

Climate change

A persistent change in the statistical characteristics of the climate system, including average temperature, precipitation patterns, atmospheric circulation and the frequency or intensity of certain extremes.

Climate change is not a new phenomenon. Long before humans existed, the Earth experienced major changes in temperature, atmospheric composition, ocean circulation and ice-sheet extent. Some of these changes unfolded over millions of years; others occurred rapidly enough to transform ecosystems within a human lifetime.

The important distinction is not simply that the climate is changing. It is what is driving that change, how quickly it is occurring, and how the different components of the Earth system are responding.

The geological record does not show us a planet with a permanently stable climate. It shows us a planet whose climate responds to changes in energy, atmosphere, oceans, ice and life.

Understanding that history is particularly important when examining modern climate change. We can compare present observations with natural records extending thousands, hundreds of thousands and, in some cases, millions of years into the past.

02 · Geological context

The Quaternary: a world shaped by ice

The Quaternary is the most recent geological period, beginning approximately 2.58 million years ago and continuing to the present day. It is the interval in which repeated glaciations profoundly reshaped continents, coastlines and ecosystems.

The period belongs to the Cenozoic Era and is conventionally divided into two epochs:

Epoch Approximate age Defining characteristics
Pleistocene 2.58 million to 11,700 years ago Repeated glacial and interglacial cycles, large continental ice sheets and major environmental change.
Holocene 11,700 years ago to present The current interglacial, during which agriculture, settled societies and complex human civilisations developed.

The Pleistocene is often associated with the familiar idea of the Ice Age. In reality, it was not one uninterrupted period of extreme cold. Instead, the climate oscillated between colder glacial conditions and warmer interglacial intervals.

During glacial periods, large ice sheets expanded across northern Europe and North America. In places such as Britain, advancing glaciers eroded bedrock, transported sediment and deposited extensive accumulations of till and glaciofluvial material.

These processes left an enormous geological legacy. Much of the landscape visible across northern Europe today reflects the influence of Quaternary glaciation: valleys, moraines, drumlins, eskers, meltwater channels and extensive superficial deposits.

The Quaternary is therefore important for more than climate science. It is fundamental to understanding landscape evolution, sedimentary environments, groundwater systems, geological hazards and the ground conditions encountered in engineering.

2.58 Ma Beginning of the Quaternary Period
11.7 ka Beginning of the Holocene Epoch
100+ m Approximate magnitude of global sea-level difference between major glacial and interglacial states

The formal geological divisions and their boundaries are maintained by the International Commission on Stratigraphy .

03 · Changing environments

Glacial and interglacial worlds

Imagine a northern Europe with vast ice sheets covering much of Britain and Scandinavia, sea levels more than 100 metres below modern levels, and landscapes dominated by cold, open environments. This was not an isolated event: conditions like these occurred repeatedly throughout the Pleistocene.

Glacial periods

A glacial is an extended interval during which global temperatures are relatively low and continental ice sheets expand.

As snowfall accumulates in high-latitude and high-altitude regions, more snow survives each summer than melts. Over time, successive layers compact into glacial ice. As ice sheets thicken, they spread outwards under their own weight.

The growth of these ice sheets has consequences far beyond the regions they cover. Water becomes stored on land as ice, lowering global sea level. Ocean circulation changes, vegetation belts shift, and habitats are reorganised.

Interglacial periods

An interglacial is a warmer interval between major glaciations. During these periods, ice sheets retreat, sea levels rise and ecosystems reorganise in response to warmer conditions.

The Holocene, which began approximately 11,700 years ago, is the current interglacial. It followed the last major deglaciation, during which large ice sheets retreated from areas of northern Europe and North America.

Importantly, interglacials are not perfectly stable. They contain regional variations in temperature, precipitation and atmospheric circulation. The distinction is that these variations occur within a broader warm interval rather than a full glacial state.

Glacial and interglacial cycles are not simply changes between cold and warm weather. They represent major reorganisations of the Earth's atmosphere, oceans, cryosphere and biosphere.
04 · The astronomical connection

Milankovitch cycles: how Earth's orbit influences climate

One of the most important discoveries in palaeoclimate science is that variations in Earth's orbit and orientation influence the distribution of solar energy reaching different parts of the planet.

These variations are known as Milankovitch cycles, named after Serbian mathematician and astronomer Milutin Milanković. They influence the timing and geographical distribution of incoming solar radiation, particularly at high northern latitudes where changes in summer insolation can influence whether winter snow survives the summer.

There are three principal components:

1. Eccentricity — the shape of Earth's orbit

Earth's orbit around the Sun is not perfectly circular. It varies between more circular and more elliptical configurations.

These changes occur over several periodicities, with an important component around 100,000 years and a longer component around 405,000 years. Eccentricity influences the strength of the precessional effect on seasonal insolation.

2. Obliquity — the tilt of Earth's axis

Earth's rotational axis is tilted relative to the plane of its orbit. This tilt varies between approximately 22.1 and 24.5 degrees over a cycle of around 41,000 years.

A greater axial tilt produces more pronounced seasonal contrasts, particularly at high latitudes. A smaller tilt produces less extreme seasonal differences.

3. Precession — the orientation of Earth's axis

Earth's rotational axis slowly changes its orientation, much like the wobble of a spinning top. This alters the position of the seasons relative to Earth's position in its orbit.

Climatically important precessional cycles occur at approximately 19,000 to 23,000 years.

Orbital parameter Main periodicity Principal influence
Eccentricity ~100,000 and 405,000 years Modulates orbital shape and the strength of precessional effects.
Obliquity ~41,000 years Changes the seasonal contrast between high and low latitudes.
Precession ~19,000–23,000 years Changes the timing of seasons relative to Earth's orbital position.

These orbital variations do not significantly change the total annual amount of solar energy received by Earth. Their importance lies in how that energy is distributed across seasons and latitudes.

In particular, relatively cool summers in the Northern Hemisphere can allow winter snow to survive, enabling ice sheets to grow over successive years. Warmer summers encourage melting and can contribute to ice-sheet retreat.

This helps explain why orbital variations are closely associated with the rhythm of Quaternary glacial cycles.

05 · The climate system

Why small changes can produce large consequences: climate feedbacks

Orbital variations help initiate changes in the climate system, but they cannot explain the full magnitude of glacial and interglacial transitions. To understand those changes, we need to consider feedbacks.

A climate feedback occurs when an initial change in the climate system triggers secondary processes that either amplify or reduce the original change.

Ice–albedo feedback

Snow and ice reflect a large proportion of incoming sunlight. This property is known as albedo.

When ice sheets expand, more sunlight is reflected back into space, reducing the energy absorbed by Earth's surface. This encourages further cooling and can promote additional ice growth.

The reverse occurs during warming: ice melts, darker land and ocean surfaces become exposed, and more solar energy is absorbed.

Greenhouse gas feedbacks

Carbon dioxide and methane influence how efficiently Earth's atmosphere retains heat. During glacial cycles, atmospheric concentrations of these gases changed alongside global temperature.

Ice-core records show that carbon dioxide concentrations were generally lower during glacial periods and higher during interglacials. Changes in ocean circulation, biological activity and carbon storage contributed to these variations.

Greenhouse gases therefore acted as important amplifiers of the initial climate changes associated with orbital forcing.

Ocean circulation

The oceans redistribute heat around the planet. Their circulation is influenced by temperature, salinity, wind and the formation of dense water masses.

Changes in freshwater input, particularly from melting ice sheets, can influence ocean density and circulation. This can alter regional climate patterns and affect the transport of heat between low and high latitudes.

Vegetation and the carbon cycle

Vegetation influences climate through carbon storage, surface reflectivity and the exchange of water between land and atmosphere.

As climate changes, forests, grasslands and tundra shift their geographical distributions. These changes can influence how much carbon is stored in soils and vegetation, creating further interactions within the climate system.

The important distinction: forcing versus feedback

A forcing initiates or drives a change in the climate system. A feedback modifies the response to that change. Orbital variations provide an important pacing mechanism for Quaternary glacial cycles, while greenhouse gases, ice, oceans and vegetation help determine their magnitude and character.

06 · Reconstructing ancient environments

Reading the climate archive: how do we know what happened?

Nobody was recording daily temperatures during the last ice age. There were no weather stations, satellite observations or atmospheric monitoring networks. So how can scientists reconstruct climate conditions from thousands or even millions of years ago?

The answer lies in natural archives.

Across the Earth, environmental materials preserve chemical, biological and physical evidence of the conditions under which they formed. These records are known as climate proxies.

A proxy does not usually provide a direct measurement of temperature or rainfall. Instead, it records something that responds to environmental conditions. Scientists use the relationship between that signal and the environment to reconstruct past climates.

Ice cores: preserving ancient atmospheres

Ice cores are among the most important archives in palaeoclimate research. They are drilled from large ice sheets and glaciers, particularly in Antarctica and Greenland.

As snow accumulates, successive layers become compacted into ice. Small bubbles of air can become trapped within the ice, preserving samples of ancient atmospheres.

By analysing these bubbles, scientists can measure past concentrations of greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane.

The oxygen and hydrogen isotope composition of the ice itself also provides information about past temperature and moisture conditions.

Ice-core records have been central to establishing the close relationship between atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations and temperature across successive glacial cycles.

Marine sediments: the ocean floor as an archive

The seabed is covered by layers of sediment that accumulate over thousands and millions of years. These deposits contain the remains of marine organisms, minerals transported from land, volcanic ash and chemical signals from the ocean.

One particularly important group of organisms is the foraminifera: microscopic, often shelled marine organisms that live in the water column or on the seabed.

Their shells preserve geochemical information about the water in which they formed.

Measurements of oxygen isotopes, particularly the ratio of oxygen-18 to oxygen-16, are widely used in palaeoceanography. These measurements can reflect a combination of ocean temperature and changes in global ice volume.

This distinction is important. An oxygen-isotope record is not automatically a direct temperature record. Its interpretation depends on the archive, the organism and the wider environmental context.

Pollen: reconstructing ancient vegetation

Plants produce pollen grains that can be transported by wind or water and preserved in lake sediments, peat deposits and other sedimentary environments.

Different plant species produce pollen with recognisable shapes and surface characteristics. By identifying and counting pollen grains in successive sediment layers, scientists can reconstruct changes in vegetation through time.

A shift from tree-dominated pollen assemblages towards grasses and cold-tolerant plants, for example, may indicate a transition towards cooler or drier conditions.

Pollen analysis is particularly valuable for understanding how terrestrial ecosystems responded to changing climate.

Speleothems: climate records hidden in caves

Stalagmites and stalactites form as mineral-rich water enters caves and deposits calcium carbonate. These deposits are collectively known as speleothems.

As a stalagmite grows, it can preserve changes in oxygen isotopes, carbon isotopes and trace elements. These signals may provide information about rainfall, moisture sources, vegetation and changes in regional climate.

Because some speleothems can be dated precisely using uranium-series methods, they are valuable archives of environmental change.

Lake sediments, biomarkers and ancient DNA

Lakes and wetlands collect material from their surrounding catchments. Their sediments can preserve pollen, plant fragments, microfossils, charcoal, minerals and organic compounds.

Some organic molecules, known as biomarkers, are produced by particular groups of organisms. Their chemical composition can provide clues about past temperature, vegetation and environmental conditions.

Sedimentary ancient DNA provides another way of identifying organisms that once inhabited an environment. Even where visible fossils are absent, fragments of genetic material can sometimes survive in sediment.

Tephra: volcanic ash as a chronological marker

Volcanic eruptions can distribute ash across enormous distances. When this material settles into lakes, peatlands, marine sediments or ice, it can form a recognisable layer within the geological record.

These deposits are known as tephra. Their volcanic glass can preserve distinctive chemical compositions, allowing scientists to compare deposits from different locations.

Where a tephra layer can be linked to a known eruption, it can provide an important chronological marker. The same volcanic event may be recorded in several geographically separated sedimentary sequences.

This is the basis of tephrochronology: using volcanic ash layers to establish correlations and improve the chronological framework of environmental records.

It is an especially useful technique because a single eruption can leave a distinctive geological fingerprint across multiple archives.

A sediment core is not simply a column of mud. It is a layered environmental record, with each horizon potentially preserving evidence of climate, vegetation, volcanic activity and landscape change.
07 · Chronology and uncertainty

Putting the past in order: how are climate records dated?

Reconstructing an ancient environment is only part of the problem. Scientists also need to establish when the evidence formed.

A climate record without a reliable chronology can be difficult to interpret. For example, identifying a major vegetation change is useful, but knowing whether it occurred during a glacial transition, a volcanic event or a period of regional warming gives that observation much greater scientific value.

Different archives require different dating techniques. Some of the most widely used include:

  • Radiocarbon dating: Measures the radioactive decay of carbon-14 in once-living material. It is particularly useful for dating organic remains from the late Quaternary, within its effective age range.
  • Uranium-series dating: Uses radioactive decay relationships within uranium-bearing minerals, including cave carbonates and corals.
  • Optically stimulated luminescence: Estimates the time since mineral grains, commonly quartz or feldspar, were last exposed to sufficient sunlight.
  • Tephrochronology: Correlates volcanic ash deposits between sedimentary sequences using their stratigraphic position and geochemical characteristics.
  • Ice-core chronology: Uses annual layer counting, volcanic horizons, chemical signals and other chronological constraints.
  • Biostratigraphy: Uses the distribution and succession of fossil organisms to establish relative ages and correlations.

These methods do not all provide the same precision or operate over the same timescales. Their suitability depends on the material being dated, the age of the deposit and the environmental context.

Scientists often combine multiple dating approaches to construct an age-depth model, which estimates the age of sediment at different depths within a core.

Such models also account for uncertainty. An age estimate is rarely an absolutely precise point in time; it is better understood as a probability distribution or an interval within which the true age is likely to fall.

08 · Major transitions

Important events in Quaternary climate history

The Quaternary is not a uniform climatic interval. It contains several major transitions that reveal how the Earth system has changed over geological timescales.

Approximately 2.58 million years ago — Beginning of the Quaternary

The formal beginning of the Quaternary marks an interval characterised by increasingly important Northern Hemisphere glaciation and major environmental fluctuations.

Approximately 1.2–0.7 million years ago — Mid-Pleistocene Transition

The dominant rhythm of glacial cycles shifted from a pattern strongly associated with approximately 41,000-year periodicity towards larger, more asymmetric cycles with a prominent approximately 100,000-year rhythm. The causes involve complex interactions between ice-sheet dynamics, orbital forcing and climate feedbacks.

Approximately 26,500–19,000 years ago — Last Glacial Maximum

Large ice sheets reached their maximum extent during the last glacial cycle. Global sea level was substantially lower than today, exposing continental shelves and connecting some landmasses.

Approximately 19,000–11,700 years ago — Deglaciation

Global temperatures rose, ice sheets retreated and sea levels increased. The transition involved complex regional changes, including episodes of abrupt climate variability.

Approximately 11,700 years ago — Beginning of the Holocene

The start of the current interglacial marks a major transition towards the warmer environmental conditions within which human societies developed.

The Mid-Pleistocene Transition

One of the most important long-term changes in Quaternary climate history is the Mid-Pleistocene Transition.

Earlier in the Pleistocene, glacial cycles were strongly influenced by approximately 41,000-year variations associated with changes in Earth's axial tilt.

During the Mid-Pleistocene Transition, glacial cycles became larger and increasingly asymmetric, with a dominant rhythm around 100,000 years.

This transition cannot be explained by a simple change in orbital forcing. Instead, researchers investigate the role of evolving ice-sheet behaviour, changing ice dynamics, ocean circulation, regolith removal and interactions between the climate system's feedback mechanisms.

The Last Glacial Maximum

The Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) represents the interval when global ice volume was at its greatest during the most recent glacial cycle.

Extensive ice sheets covered large parts of northern North America and northern Europe. Britain and Ireland experienced major glacial activity, with ice advancing across substantial areas of the landscape.

The growth of continental ice sheets locked up enormous quantities of water, resulting in global sea levels more than 120 metres below modern levels.

This transformed coastlines, altered migration routes and exposed areas of continental shelf that are now submerged.

Deglaciation and abrupt climate change

The transition out of the last glacial period was not a smooth, uninterrupted warming trend.

The deglaciation included periods of relatively rapid warming and cooling. One particularly well-known event is the Younger Dryas, a return to colder conditions in parts of the North Atlantic region approximately 12,900 to 11,700 years ago.

Its expression varied geographically, and its causes remain an important area of palaeoclimate research. Changes in freshwater input and North Atlantic ocean circulation are central to many explanations.

09 · The last 11,700 years

The Holocene: the world in which civilisation developed

The Holocene is the current geological epoch. Beginning approximately 11,700 years ago, it followed the final transition out of the last glacial period.

Compared with the dramatic glacial cycles of the Pleistocene, the Holocene has generally experienced relatively limited global temperature variability.

This does not mean that climate remained unchanged. Regional temperatures, rainfall patterns, monsoon systems and ocean circulation all experienced significant variations.

The early and middle Holocene saw substantial changes in vegetation and ecosystems as glaciers retreated and forests expanded into areas previously dominated by cold-adapted environments.

These environmental conditions formed the setting for the development of agriculture, settled communities and increasingly complex human societies.

Holocene climate variability

Several periods of regional climatic change are particularly well documented.

  • Holocene Thermal Maximum: A period of relatively warm conditions in many northern high-latitude regions, although its timing and magnitude varied geographically.
  • Medieval Climate Anomaly: A period of regionally variable climate conditions, often associated with relatively warm conditions in parts of the North Atlantic region.
  • Little Ice Age: A period of widespread, though geographically uneven, cooling particularly evident in parts of the Northern Hemisphere between approximately the 14th and 19th centuries.

These intervals are useful reminders that climate variability is geographically complex. A warm period in one region does not necessarily represent simultaneous global warming, and the same applies to periods of cooling.

10 · The modern climate

Modern climate change in the context of deep time

The geological record demonstrates that Earth's climate has undergone enormous natural changes. However, recognising natural climate variability does not explain away modern warming. In fact, understanding past climate behaviour helps us identify what makes the present situation so unusual.

Since the Industrial Revolution, atmospheric concentrations of greenhouse gases have increased substantially. The principal driver is human activity, particularly the combustion of coal, oil and natural gas, alongside land-use change and industrial processes.

Carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide absorb outgoing infrared radiation. Increasing their concentrations changes the Earth's energy balance, resulting in a warming climate system.

This is not simply a correlation between rising carbon dioxide and temperature. The physical mechanisms are understood, the expected pattern of atmospheric warming has been observed, and multiple independent lines of evidence identify human influence as the dominant cause of recent global warming.

What does the geological record tell us about CO₂?

Ice cores provide direct records of ancient atmospheric composition by preserving tiny samples of air trapped within compacted snow and ice.

Across the late Quaternary, carbon dioxide concentrations varied substantially between glacial and interglacial conditions. Over much of the last 800,000 years, concentrations generally ranged from roughly 180 parts per million during glacial periods to around 280 parts per million during interglacials, with some variation beyond those approximate limits.

These records reveal a close relationship between atmospheric carbon dioxide and temperature across successive glacial cycles.

The modern situation is different. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report reported that atmospheric carbon dioxide reached approximately 410 parts per million in 2019, higher than at any time in at least two million years according to the evidence assessed.

The significance is not just the concentration itself. It is also the speed at which the atmosphere has changed.

How unusual is modern warming?

The IPCC assessed global surface temperature during 2011–2020 as approximately 1.1°C above the 1850–1900 reference period.

Its assessment also concluded that human activities, principally through greenhouse gas emissions, have unequivocally caused global warming.

The rate of recent warming is particularly important. The IPCC found that global surface temperature has increased faster since 1970 than during any other 50-year period over at least the last 2,000 years.

Comparisons with much older geological intervals require care because the available archives, chronological precision and climate conditions differ. Nevertheless, the evidence from ice cores, sediments, instrumental records and climate modelling provides a consistent picture of rapid, predominantly human-driven warming.

Why natural climate change is not an alternative explanation

Natural factors such as volcanic activity, solar variability and internal ocean-atmosphere processes continue to influence climate. However, they cannot explain the observed pattern and magnitude of modern warming. Human greenhouse gas emissions provide the dominant explanation.

11 · Humans as a geological force

The Anthropocene: are humans changing the geological record?

Human activity is now altering the atmosphere, oceans, ecosystems and sedimentary environments on a global scale. This has led scientists to ask whether humanity has become a geological force in its own right.

The term Anthropocene is widely used to describe the period of substantial human influence on the Earth system.

Its proposed geological significance comes from the increasingly widespread evidence of human activity preserved in the geological record.

Examples include:

  • Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.
  • Artificial radionuclides associated with nuclear weapons testing.
  • Plastics and other synthetic materials entering sedimentary environments.
  • Large-scale changes in sediment transport and land surfaces caused by agriculture, mining and urbanisation.
  • Major changes in biodiversity and the distribution of species.

However, there is an important distinction between using the Anthropocene as a scientific concept and recognising it as a formal unit of geological time.

In March 2024, the International Union of Geological Sciences and International Commission on Stratigraphy approved the rejection of the proposal to formalise the Anthropocene as a new geological epoch.

The Holocene therefore remains the formally recognised current epoch in the Geological Time Scale.

This decision does not mean that human influence on the Earth system is insignificant or disputed. The Anthropocene remains a widely used and valuable concept across Earth and environmental sciences.

It highlights a fundamental question: how will the environmental changes taking place today be represented in the geological record of the future?

12 · Bringing the evidence together

Why understanding the past matters

The Quaternary geological record provides one of the most detailed accounts of environmental change available to Earth scientists.

Through ice cores, marine sediments, lake deposits, pollen, speleothems and volcanic ash layers, researchers can reconstruct how the Earth system responded to changing conditions over thousands and millions of years.

These records reveal a climate system that is dynamic, interconnected and capable of substantial change. They demonstrate how orbital forcing, greenhouse gases, ocean circulation, ice sheets and ecosystems interact to shape the world around us.

But the geological record also provides an important perspective on the present. Modern climate change is occurring rapidly, and the evidence identifies human greenhouse gas emissions as its dominant cause.

Understanding Earth's history does not provide a perfect prediction of the future. It does, however, give us the evidence needed to understand climate sensitivity, environmental thresholds and the consequences of changing the composition of the atmosphere.

The past is not simply a record of what the Earth used to be. It is evidence that helps us understand the processes shaping the world we live in today.

The more carefully we investigate that evidence, the better equipped we are to understand the challenges facing the Earth system.
