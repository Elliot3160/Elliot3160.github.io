Exploring 2.6 million years of Earth's climate history

Introduction to Climate Change

Before climate change can be understood, we must first establish the difference between weather and climate. Weather is characterised by changes in parameters including temperature, daylight and precipitation which can be dictated by diurnal cycles. In contrast, climate is the long term average of weather. Therefore, climate change is simply the deviation from past long term average trends in weather.

Climate change occurs both naturally and as a direct result of human activity. The reason why we can convincingly argue that humans are directly responsible for climate change is made evident in the geological record. The Quaternary period (present day to 2.6 million years ago) is especially valuable for this. It allows us to pin point, using high resolution 'climate proxies', the moment at which humans began to drive the climate away from its long term trends, which were previously controlled by natural mechanisms. A whole suite of geological, geographical and palaeontological evidence is available to support this notion.

The Quaternary Period

Spanning the last 2.6 million years, the Quaternary is marked by repeated advance and retreat of major ice sheets, significant shifts in ecosystems, and the rise of modern humans.

Glacial–Interglacial Cycles

Driven by orbital variations and amplified by climate feedbacks, these cycles define much of Earth’s recent climate behaviour.

How We Know: Climate Proxies

  • Ice cores
  • Marine sediments
  • Speleothems
  • Pollen records
  • Ancient DNA

Major Climate Events of the Quaternary

Key events include the Mid-Pleistocene Transition, the Last Glacial Maximum, deglaciation phases, Holocene climate variability, and the onset of the Anthropocene.

Today's Climate in Quaternary Context

Comparing modern changes to past climates helps us understand the scale and speed of current warming relative to natural variability.
