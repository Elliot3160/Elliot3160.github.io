The Ground Between the Boreholes

Boreholes provide essential evidence, but they do not describe the whole ground. Ground risk often sits in the spaces between data points — where geology, history, groundwater, human activity and uncertainty have to be interpreted together.

Technical insight Ground risk Ground models & interpretation By Elliot May

The central argument

Ground risk is not just a borehole problem. Boreholes are direct observations, but they are still only points in space. A reliable ground model depends on how those points are connected using geological reasoning, site history, geomorphology, groundwater evidence and engineering judgement.

Boreholes are one of the most important sources of information in ground investigation. They provide direct evidence of ground conditions, allow samples to be recovered, support in-situ testing and give engineers a physical record of what was encountered at specific locations.

But boreholes do not describe the whole ground. They describe the ground at discrete points. Between those points, the ground still has to be interpreted.

This is where ground risk becomes more complicated. The most important question is not simply what was found in a borehole, but how that evidence fits into the wider site context. Ground conditions are shaped by geology, geomorphology, groundwater, weathering, historical land use, earthworks, buried structures, mining, contamination, drainage and uncertainty. None of those factors can be understood properly from isolated logs alone.

Core principle

Boreholes are evidence points, not the ground model itself. The ground model is built in the interpretation between them.

Boreholes are evidence points, not reality

A borehole gives valuable direct information, but it also has limits. It is a narrow vertical record through a complex three-dimensional environment. It can confirm what is present at one location, but it cannot automatically explain how that material changes laterally across a site.

This matters because the ground is rarely uniform. Made ground can vary significantly over short distances. Superficial deposits may reflect former channels, glacial processes, slope movement or localised reworking. Bedrock can be weathered, fractured or altered differently across a site. Groundwater conditions may change with season, permeability, topography and artificial drainage.

If boreholes are treated as isolated records, there is a risk that interpretation becomes too mechanical. A layer encountered at one location may be assumed to continue evenly across the site. A boundary may be drawn too confidently. A localised obstruction, soft pocket or weathered zone may be overlooked because it does not fit neatly into a simple stratigraphic table.

This does not reduce the value of boreholes. It increases the importance of interpreting them carefully. The value of a borehole is not only in what it records, but in how that record is connected to the wider evidence base.

Desk study is not admin

The desk study is sometimes treated as a preliminary or administrative stage before the “real” investigation begins. In practice, it is one of the first stages of interpretation.

Historical maps, geological maps, mining records, aerial imagery, topographic data, drainage information, previous ground investigations and environmental records can all shape the ground model before intrusive work starts. They help identify what questions the investigation needs to answer and where uncertainty may be concentrated.

A borehole log may record made ground. A desk study may explain why that made ground exists. A trial pit may encounter buried brick and ash. Historical mapping may show former buildings, yards, pits, railway land or industrial use. A borehole may encounter soft alluvium. Geomorphology and topography may reveal the former channel system that explains why it is there.

Without this context, factual records can become disconnected observations. With context, they become part of a developing ground model.

Interpretive value

A desk study does not simply provide background information. It frames the technical questions that the ground investigation is trying to answer.

Geology is spatial

Ground models are spatial interpretations. They are not just lists of strata, laboratory results or exploratory hole records. They represent how ground conditions are expected to vary across a site and how those variations may affect design, construction and risk.

This is especially important in complex superficial geology. Glacial deposits, river terraces, alluvium, peat, colluvium and made ground can be highly variable. Boundaries may be irregular. Material descriptions may change gradually rather than abruptly. Units may interfinger, pinch out or be locally absent.

The same applies to rock. Weathering profiles, fracture zones, faulting, lithological variation, groundwater movement and discontinuities can all influence the engineering behaviour of bedrock. A rockhead surface is not just a line on a section. It is an interpreted surface with uncertainty attached to it.

Treating geology spatially means asking how each observation relates to the wider pattern. Does the stratum make sense in relation to topography? Does the logged material match the mapped geology? Are changes between boreholes gradual, abrupt or unexplained? Are there historical or geomorphological reasons for local variation? Where should uncertainty remain visible?

A ground model should not hide uncertainty. It should organise it in a way that helps technical decisions.

Risk lives in the gaps

Many ground risks are not created by what is confidently known. They are created by what is uncertain, discontinuous, poorly constrained or assumed.

A buried obstruction may sit between exploratory locations. A former excavation may be missed if historical evidence is not reviewed carefully. A soft zone may be localised. A groundwater pathway may not be obvious from a single monitoring record. A contamination source may be associated with a former land use rather than the material encountered in one exploratory hole.

This is why the gaps between boreholes matter. The ground model has to make reasoned interpretations across those gaps, while also being honest about where confidence is lower.

The aim is not to eliminate uncertainty completely. That is rarely possible. The aim is to understand uncertainty well enough that it can be managed. A good ground model helps identify which risks are reasonably constrained, which require further investigation and which should be carried forward into design or construction planning.

Risk principle

Ground risk is often controlled by the relationship between observations, not by any single observation in isolation.

From factual data to integrated ground models

A stronger ground model brings different sources of evidence together. Borehole logs, trial pit records, laboratory testing, groundwater monitoring, geological mapping, historical records, topography, photographs and previous investigations should not sit as separate fragments. They should form a connected interpretation.

This is where the link to data workflows becomes important. If factual information is structured, traceable and consistent, it becomes easier to compare observations across a site. If records are fragmented, the interpretive process becomes slower and less transparent.

Integrated ground models depend on both technical judgement and good information management. The judgement is needed to interpret geology, assess uncertainty and understand engineering significance. The workflow is needed to keep the evidence connected enough for that judgement to be applied clearly.

A borehole log can tell us what was encountered. A laboratory result can tell us something about material behaviour. A groundwater record can show a condition at a point in time. Historical mapping can suggest why certain materials or hazards may be present. The ground model is where these separate records become a coherent explanation of the site.

The direction of travel

The future of ground investigation is likely to involve more digital tools, better data structures, increased use of GIS, improved AGS workflows, three-dimensional modelling and selective automation. These developments are valuable, but they do not remove the need for geological interpretation.

In fact, they may make interpretation more important. As more information becomes available, the challenge becomes deciding what that information means, how reliable it is, where it conflicts and how it should influence the ground model.

Digital tools can help organise and visualise ground information. They can support consistency checks, improve traceability and make spatial patterns easier to interrogate. But the underlying technical question remains the same: what is the ground likely to be doing between the points we have observed?

That question cannot be answered by boreholes alone. It requires context, reasoning and a willingness to treat uncertainty as part of the model rather than an inconvenience to be removed.

Conclusion

Boreholes are essential, but they are not the whole story. They provide direct evidence of ground conditions at specific locations. The ground model is built by connecting those observations through geological, historical, hydrogeological and engineering interpretation.

The most important risks may not sit neatly inside a single exploratory hole record. They may sit between boreholes, in the assumptions made across the site, in the uncertainty around boundaries, or in the wider context that explains why the ground varies.

Better ground models come from recognising this. They require good factual data, strong workflows and careful professional judgement. Most importantly, they require us to remember that the ground is spatial, variable and uncertain.

Boreholes show us points of truth.
Ground models help us understand the space between them.
